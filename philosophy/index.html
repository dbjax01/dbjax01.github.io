Teaching philosophy

Students learn the most when they are making something real, for people who will see it, with support shaped to who they are. I build my classroom around that idea.

The shape of a project in my room

Shown here for a small-business unit.

  1. Ask. Students start with a real problem and the people who have it.
  2. Research. They study customers and costs using real data.
  3. Build. They put a plan on paper: costs, prices, break-even.
  4. Present. They pitch to an audience that asks hard questions.
  5. Reflect. They name what they assumed and what turned out wrong.

Projects, not worksheets

A skill sticks when students need it to finish something.

A skill with no purpose is easy to forget. When students need a spreadsheet formula to finish their budget, or a design tool to finish a video for a real audience, they learn it faster and they remember why it exists. So I build each project to need the skill I just taught.

Projects are not a break from rigor. At Kanapaha Middle School, students finished one or two a week, and each Excel assignment built on the last, ending with a payroll register that used everything they had learned. Projects ran alongside certification practice tests because the two do different jobs. The project shows students what a skill is for. The practice shows them whether they have it.

What it looks like
  • Students choose the career, city, and car behind their budget, so the numbers are theirs.
  • Work goes to a real audience when I can find one. A nonprofit’s essay contest connected one student with a marine biologist.
  • Many projects end in a presentation, so students practice explaining their choices.

Same goal, different routes

Every student gets a clear target and a path that fits.

Students arrive with different backgrounds, different skills, and different reasons to care. Differentiation does not mean lowering the bar. It means keeping the target the same and changing the route. I make the standard clear, then give students choices in how they get there and how they show what they know.

At Ocala Youth Academy, every student followed an individual, standards-based path. One student could be in Algebra 1-A while another worked on Geometry, and many arrived with large gaps in what they knew. That taught me to find out where a student is before deciding where to start, and to make the next step small enough that a student believes they can take it.

What it looks like
  • Interactive lessons where students answer, match, and draw, so every student responds and not only the ones who raise a hand.
  • Choice inside a shared standard. Students pick the topic or design, and everyone meets the same skills.
  • Room to go further. A few Kanapaha students loved programming, and the class gave them space to dive in.

Everyone belongs, and everyone can get in

If one student cannot reach the lesson, the lesson needs work.

I design lessons so more students can get in the first time. In Peru, I learned that some women in my groups could not read. I rebuilt my lessons to be more visual, collaborative, and hands-on, and they worked better for everyone, including the participants who read well. A change that opens the door for one learner often helps the whole room.

Inclusion also means students know what to expect and see themselves in the work. Posted routines and clear expectations support students who need structure and cost the others nothing. At Ocala I taught students who were receiving mental health services, and I learned how much steadiness matters. My coursework in differentiated instruction, ESOL, and teaching diverse populations gave me the tools. Working in a second language every day in Peru keeps me honest about what it is like to be the person who does not yet understand everything.

What it looks like
  • Visuals, hands-on work, and partner work alongside text.
  • Routines and expectations posted so students always know what comes next.
  • Heritage-month projects where students research Asian American and Pacific Islander, Black, and women’s history and share it with the class.

Know them, then expect a lot

High expectations land when students trust the person setting them.

Students rise to expectations when they believe the person setting them is on their side. So I learn what each student wants and connect the work to it. At Ocala, I framed school as a way to reach their own goals. I spent half of each day with the same group for six months to a year, and that time is what made trust possible.

Small things add up. After class each day, I called one parent with something good about their child. One mother told me my class had helped her shy daughter make new friends. I had called to tell her about a great presentation. Trust like that makes it easier to ask a student for one more revision.

What it looks like
  • One positive call home every day.
  • The Golden Rule and six non-negotiables, posted and used the same way every day.
  • Paper certificates handed out in class, because students wanted something to hold.

Make growth visible

Students should know where they stand and how to move forward.

Students work harder when they can see progress. At Kanapaha, practice tests closely matched the real certification exams and returned periodically, so students watched their scores climb. More than 150 certifications were earned in one year. Grades were point based and entered weekly, so nothing came as a surprise.

I also want students to look back. They set SMART goals, wrote a Future Email scheduled to arrive years later, and built an e-portfolio of their work. Reflection turns a finished project into something students can use on the next one.

Questions a principal might ask

Straight answers, drawn from my classrooms.

A student asks, “Why do I need to know this?”

I treat it as a fair question. At Ocala, I connected school to each student’s own goals. At Kanapaha, the budget project started with the career, city, and car a student actually wanted. When the numbers belong to the student, the answer shows up on the page.

How do you run your classroom?

With routines students can predict. At Kanapaha, the room ran on the Golden Rule and six non-negotiables, and routines were posted so students always knew what to expect. Every class opened the same way: five minutes of typing practice while I took attendance.

How do you teach students who are at very different levels?

I keep the target the same and change the route. At Ocala, every student followed an individual, standards-based path across nine courses, from Algebra 1-A to Marine Science. In Peru, I rebuilt lessons when I learned some adults could not read. I plan a first version for the whole room, then adjust it for the people who need something different.

How do you grade project-based work fairly?

I tie each project to a specific skill and tell students up front what that skill looks like when it is done well. That way the grade reflects what a student can do, not only how polished the result looks. Grades are point based and entered weekly, and practice tests show students how their skills are growing.

How do you work with families?

Families hear from me when things go well, not only when something goes wrong. A year later, a former student wrote to tell me she was already earning volunteer hours toward the Bright Futures Scholarship, which we had studied in class.

What are you still working on?

Two things. I am not yet certified in Family and Consumer Sciences or statistics, though both interest me. And my youth entrepreneurship work in Peru has only just begun, so I am still learning what students can build when they have a real audience. I want to learn from the teachers I work beside, too.
